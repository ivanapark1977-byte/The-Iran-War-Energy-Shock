Key Findings

1

The 2026 US-Israeli war on Iran has triggered the largest supply disruption in the history of the global oil market. The Strait of Hormuz closure and Iranian missile strikes on Ras Laffan Industrial City have simultaneously caused a historic price spike and structurally reshaped the long-term LNG supply-demand balance — paradoxically accelerating the conditions for a prolonged oversupply glut that will penalise most projects sanctioned between 2023 and 2026.

2

The war simultaneously pushed LNG prices to crisis highs and destroyed the demand base that the incoming capacity wave was built to serve. Price-sensitive South and Southeast Asian markets are now accelerating coal switching and renewable leapfrogging, creating permanent demand destruction.

3

The 666 mtpa of nameplate capacity expected online by 2028 will create the most severe structural LNG surplus in history. Implied utilisation rates will fall to 62% at peak, with the JKM trough reaching $7.54/MMBtu in 2031 — below the all-in breakeven of at least nine of the 24 major projects reviewed.

4

Qatar's competitive position is paradoxically preserved in the long run. Its $3.80–3.90/MMBtu breakeven ensures viability at any price point — but North Field East and West delays caused by the war mean Qatari market share is permanently ceded to US Gulf Coast producers who face no Hormuz risk.

Section 01

War Impact on Energy Markets

The conflict generated an immediate supply shock of historic magnitude. The IEA characterised the disruption as the "greatest global energy security challenge in history." The Strait of Hormuz closure removed 20 million barrels per day of crude oil flows and approximately 20% of global LNG trade from the market in a matter of days.

Oil Market

Brent crude surged from a pre-war level of approximately $65/bbl to over $120/bbl following the Strait closure, with physical Dubai prices rising 76% — more than double the paper-market Brent move. By early April, analysts were modelling a potential $200/bbl scenario if the Strait remained closed through summer and strategic reserves were exhausted.

LNG Market

The LNG market absorbed a structurally more damaging shock than oil due to the physical concentration of export capacity at a single facility. Unlike oil, where alternative producers could theoretically increase output, there is no spare LNG liquefaction capacity available at short notice to replace Qatari volumes.

Section 02

Long-Term Demand Destruction (2025–2050)

The war's demand-side effects extend far beyond the immediate crisis period. Three structural mechanisms are reshaping long-run energy demand in ways that will outlast the conflict by decades.

Southeast Asia LNG "Skip" Effect

The most consequential long-term demand consequence is the acceleration of what analysts had already flagged as a risk: price-sensitive markets in South and Southeast Asia choosing to bypass LNG as a transition fuel entirely. Bangladesh, Pakistan, Thailand, and the Philippines — all priced out of the spot market at $21–25/MMBtu — are accelerating renewable deployment and coal switching, with governments explicitly revising national energy plans away from LNG dependency.

Critical Insight: The IEA's Own Numbers Indict the Market. The IEA's Stated Policies Scenario projects total LNG trade in 2050 at approximately 354 mtpa. The 666 mtpa of nameplate liquefaction capacity arriving by 2028 already exceeds this 2050 demand projection by 88%. Even under Scenario A demand recovery, the implied utilisation rate at peak capacity is approximately 62% — a level that destroys the economics of the marginal project.

Source-Level War Impact Narrative

Natural gas and LNG carry the largest absolute war-related loss: −13 EJ by 2035 and −17 EJ by 2050. The mechanism is the permanent repricing of Hormuz chokepoint risk combined with the LNG skip dynamic in South and Southeast Asia.

Coal shows a split trajectory — a temporary 2–5 EJ surge in 2026–2030 as Japan, South Korea, and India substitute for lost LNG, followed by a 7–12 EJ decline against baseline from 2035 as the war accelerates coal retirement policy.

Wind and Solar is the largest beneficiary, running 12–24 EJ above baseline by 2040–2050. Nuclear accelerates by 3–5 EJ above baseline from 2035. Oil absorbs a moderate −3 to −8 EJ demand shock driven by stagflation, aviation disruption, and GDP drag.

Section 03

Global LNG Capacity Build-Out

The global LNG industry is simultaneously experiencing its worst demand shock and its largest supply wave. Between 2025 and 2030, approximately 345 bcm/yr (~253 mtpa) of new LNG export capacity is expected to come online from projects already at FID and under construction — the largest capacity wave in LNG history.

The US Structural Dominance

The United States has been the dominant driver of LNG FIDs since 2019, accounting for more than 55% of sanctioned capacity. US projects carry a structural competitive advantage in the post-war environment: no Hormuz exposure, HH-linked pricing, and strong political tailwinds. By 2030, the US will account for approximately 33–35% of global LNG production — making Henry Hub the effective global price setter for the first time in LNG history.

Qatar Timeline Slippage: The Three-Front Resource Problem

The March 2026 Iranian missile strikes have created three competing demands on a finite pool of engineering contractors, long-lead equipment, and capital — at the same time as revenue has been severely curtailed by the production halt. The rational sequencing is repair first, selective hardening in parallel, and expansion second. But even this triage cannot fully resolve the underlying resource conflict, and slippage across all expansion timelines is now a near-certainty.

The fundamental constraint is not capital — Qatar has deep fiscal reserves — but engineering bandwidth. The EPC contractor market (Chiyoda, Technip Energies, CCC) was already the binding constraint before March 2026. The strikes have not created new contractor capacity; they have created new demand competing for the same scarce resources.

Three Simultaneous Tracks

  • Track 1 — Repair: Repairing Trains 4 and 6 requires the same EPC contractors currently committed to NFE and NFS construction. Main cryogenic heat exchangers likely require full replacement — not repair — from a global supplier base already at capacity. This track takes priority by necessity: it represents restoration of existing revenue, not future optionality.
  • Track 2 — North Field Expansion: Construction cannot resume offshore while the Strait of Hormuz remains closed to insured commercial traffic. Final commissioning onshore requires international technical staff who have largely evacuated. The NFE first train has slipped from H2 2026 to late 2027 at the earliest; NFE full capacity pushed to 2028–2029; NFS to 2029–2030; North Field West startup now projected at 2031.
  • Track 3 — Hardening: Comprehensive air defense coverage of Ras Laffan — 4–6 battery systems (Patriot, THAAD or equivalent) — carries an estimated upfront cost of $500–750 million plus $50–100 million annually in operating costs. The binding constraint is not money but procurement and integration timelines of 2–4 years, compounded by geopolitically complex US FMS approvals.

Strategic Consequences of Slippage

The timeline slippage carries three compounding strategic consequences beyond the immediate revenue loss of up to $20 billion per year during the repair period.

First, market share displacement is structural, not temporary. The NFE capacity scheduled to come online in 2026–2028 was already contracted to long-term buyers including Sinopec, Eni, Shell, TotalEnergies, and CNPC. Force majeure on these contracts triggers replacement sourcing — primarily from US Gulf Coast producers. Buyers who secure replacement supply under long-term contracts will not automatically return to Qatari supply when Ras Laffan recovers.

Second, QatarEnergy's internal project management bandwidth — its most constrained non-capital resource — will be consumed by repair coordination and contractor management for at least the first 18–24 months. NFE and NFS expansion projects will suffer from reduced senior oversight regardless of capital allocation decisions.

Third, North Field West arrives into the deepest part of the oversupply trough. The 2031 startup date implied by the slippage analysis coincides almost precisely with the projected JKM trough of $7.54/MMBtu. At Qatar's $3.90/MMBtu breakeven this remains commercially viable, but the strategic logic of the project — capturing premium LNG pricing as the largest low-cost producer — is seriously weakened when the market is absorbing a 255 mtpa surplus.

The Coal Floor Mechanism: The JKM oversupply trough is bounded from below by seaborne coal pricing (~$4.00–4.50/MMBtu Newcastle equivalent by 2031). When LNG prices fall within $1–2/MMBtu of coal, fuel-switching demand from power generators provides a demand floor that arrests further price decline. This mechanism sets the effective JKM floor at approximately $6.00–6.50/MMBtu.

Henry Hub as Global Anchor

As the US accounts for 35% of global LNG supply by 2030, Henry Hub transitions from a purely domestic benchmark to the effective marginal cost signal for the entire global LNG market. RBAC/G2M2 modelling shows the HH–JKM spread narrowing to just $2.55/MMBtu at the 2031 trough — insufficient to cover transportation and regasification costs for many cargoes, creating a structural period of cargo cancellations among US producers.

Section 06

Strategic Implications

For Qatar / QatarEnergy

Qatar's competitive fundamentals are intact — its $3.80–3.90/MMBtu breakeven is structurally unassailable — but the war has inflicted three compounding strategic setbacks. First, NFE arrives into a glut rather than the tight market it was timed for. Second, permanent Ras Laffan damage has ceded 12.8 mtpa of contracted volumes to spot market competitors, primarily US Gulf Coast producers. Third, the Hormuz concentration risk has become permanently priced into long-term contract negotiations.

The three-front resource problem means that even Qatar's considerable engineering capacity cannot resolve all tracks simultaneously — slippage is now structural, not discretionary.

For US LNG Producers

The war is a near-term windfall and a medium-term liability. Spot revenues during the 2026 crisis are exceptional. But projects with first gas in 2029–2031 — Woodside Louisiana, CP2, Rio Grande T4-5, Port Arthur Ph2 — arrive precisely at the trough of the oversupply cycle with insufficient contracted cover. The HH–JKM spread at the 2031 trough ($2.55/MMBtu) is insufficient to cover variable costs for unhedged cargoes, triggering cargo cancellations analogous to the 2020 COVID-period shut-ins.

For Emerging Market Buyers

The war has permanently altered the LNG risk calculus for price-sensitive importers. Countries like Bangladesh, Pakistan, and Thailand — priced out at $21–25/MMBtu during the crisis — are now designing energy systems that minimise LNG dependency. The "LNG as a bridge fuel" narrative has been structurally undermined. This demand destruction is largely irreversible: infrastructure built around coal or renewables does not revert to LNG when prices fall.

For Project Developers

The viability analysis yields three clear conclusions. Projects with first gas before 2028 capture crisis-era pricing before the glut arrives. Projects with first gas in 2029–2031 and limited contracted cover face genuine viability risk during the trough. Projects targeting first gas post-2033 should not proceed without transformative cost reduction or policy-backed offtake structures.

Glossary

Key Terms

bcm
Billion cubic metres. Standard unit for natural gas volume. 1 mtpa ≈ 1.36 bcm.
EJ
Exajoule. One quintillion (10¹⁸) joules. Used to measure total primary energy supply.
FID
Final Investment Decision. The point at which a project is formally sanctioned and capital committed.
Force Majeure
Legal provision allowing contract suspension due to unforeseeable circumstances beyond parties' control.
HH
Henry Hub. Natural gas pricing point in Louisiana; US benchmark for gas futures.
JKM
Japan Korea Marker. Asia-Pacific LNG spot price benchmark published by S&P Global Platts.
mb/d
Million barrels per day. Standard unit for crude oil production and consumption.
MMBtu
Million British thermal units. Standard unit for pricing natural gas and LNG.
mtpa
Million tonnes per annum. Standard unit for LNG production capacity.
NFE / NFS / NFW
North Field East / South / West. Qatar's three-phase LNG expansion project, totalling 126 mtpa.
Ras Laffan
Industrial city in Qatar hosting the world's largest LNG export complex (77 mtpa pre-war).
STEPS
Stated Policies Scenario. IEA's baseline projection assuming existing policies continue.
TTF
Title Transfer Facility. European natural gas price benchmark (Dutch gas hub).
